Consider the integral

$$
I=\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x(1+x^2)}\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

The apparent singularity at the origin is removable, since the integrand
tends to $1$ as $x\to0$. At infinity, its absolute value is bounded by
$x^{-3}$, so the integral converges absolutely.

We will evaluate this integral in three ways, using successively fewer
parameter derivatives: twice to obtain a differential equation, once to
obtain a cosine integral, and finally not at all, by applying the residue
theorem directly. The details justifying differentiation and the Dirichlet
integral are collected at the end.

## 1. Differentiate twice: a differential equation

For the first two methods, define

$$
F(a)=\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin(ax)}{x(1+x^2)}\,\mathrm{d}x,
\qquad a\geq0.
$$

Our target is $I=F(1)$. Differentiating once cancels the factor $1/x$:

$$
F'(a)=\int_0^\infty\frac{\cos(ax)}{1+x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

The two initial values are therefore

$$
\boxed{
F(0)=0,
\qquad
F'(0^+)=\int_0^\infty\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{1+x^2}
=\frac{\pi}{2}.
}
$$

Differentiate once more. For $a>0$,

$$
F''(a)=-\int_0^\infty\frac{x\sin(ax)}{1+x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

This second differentiation needs a separate justification: the new
integral is only conditionally convergent. We will establish that step
below. First, notice the algebraic identity

$$
\frac{x}{1+x^2}
=\frac1x-\frac{1}{x(1+x^2)}.
$$

Substituting it into the expression for $F''$ gives

$$
F''(a)
=-\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin(ax)}x\,\mathrm{d}x
+\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin(ax)}{x(1+x^2)}\,\mathrm{d}x=-\frac{\pi}{2}+F(a).
$$

Here we used the **Dirichlet integral**,

$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin(ax)}x\,\mathrm{d}x
=\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin t}{t}\,\mathrm{d}t
=\frac{\pi}{2},\qquad a>0.
$$

The substitution $t=ax$ explains why its value is independent of positive
$a$. A real-variable derivation of that value is included below, so this
method does not depend on the residue calculation.

We have obtained the initial-value problem

$$
\boxed{
F''(a)-F(a)=-\frac{\pi}{2},\quad a>0;
\qquad F(0)=0,\quad F'(0^+)=\frac{\pi}{2}.
}
$$

The homogeneous equation has solutions $e^a$ and $e^{-a}$, while the
constant $\pi/2$ is a particular solution. Hence

$$
F(a)=\frac{\pi}{2}+C_1e^a+C_2e^{-a}.
$$

Applying the two initial conditions gives

$$
C_1+C_2=-\frac{\pi}{2},
\qquad
C_1-C_2=\frac{\pi}{2}.
$$

Therefore $C_1=0$ and $C_2=-\pi/2$, giving

$$
\boxed{
F(a)=\frac{\pi}{2}(1-e^{-a}),\qquad a\geq0.
}
$$

In particular, $I=\frac{\pi}{2}(1-e^{-1})$.

## 2. Differentiate once: the residue theorem

We can also stop after the first derivative and evaluate $F'(a)$ by
residues. Consider the complex function

$$
f_a(z)=\frac{e^{iaz}}{1+z^2},\qquad a\geq0.
$$

For $R>1$, integrate over the positively oriented contour $C_R$ formed by
the real segment from $-R$ to $R$ and the upper semicircle

$$
\gamma_R(\theta)=Re^{i\theta},\qquad 0\leq\theta\leq\pi.
$$

<img src="/assets/img/blog_figures/20260909_sine_integral_contour.svg" alt="Counterclockwise upper semicircular contour: the real segment runs from minus R to R, and the arc returns from R to minus R. The pole at i is enclosed; the pole at minus i lies outside." style="width: 82%; max-width: 900px; height: auto; display: block; margin: 1.2rem auto;">

The poles are at $z=\pm i$, and only $z=i$ lies inside $C_R$. Its residue is

$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=i} f_a(z)
=\lim_{z\to i}\frac{e^{iaz}}{z+i}
=\frac{e^{-a}}{2i}.
$$

The residue theorem therefore gives

$$
\int_{-R}^R\frac{e^{iax}}{1+x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x
+\int_{\gamma_R}\frac{e^{iaz}}{1+z^2}\,\mathrm{d}z
=\pi e^{-a}.
$$

To remove the arc contribution, observe that on the upper semicircle

$$
|e^{iaz}|=e^{-a\operatorname{Im}z}\leq1,
\qquad
|1+z^2|\geq R^2-1.
$$

Since the arc has length $\pi R$,

$$
\left|
\int_{\gamma_R}\frac{e^{iaz}}{1+z^2}\,\mathrm{d}z
\right|
\leq\frac{\pi R}{R^2-1}
\longrightarrow0.
$$

This estimate works even at $a=0$; no exponential decay is needed there.
Letting $R\to\infty$, taking real parts, and using the evenness of the
cosine integrand yields

$$
2F'(a)
=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(ax)}{1+x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x
=\pi e^{-a}.
$$

Finally, integrate with respect to $a$ and use $F(0)=0$:

$$
F(a)=\int_0^a\frac{\pi}{2}e^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t
=\frac{\pi}{2}(1-e^{-a}).
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{
I=F(1)=\frac{\pi}{2}\left(1-\frac1e\right).
}
$$

## 3. No differentiation: a direct contour integral

We can avoid the parameter altogether by choosing

$$
g(z)=\frac{e^{iz}-1}{z(1+z^2)}.
$$

For real $x$, its imaginary part is exactly the original integrand:

$$
\operatorname{Im}g(x)=\frac{\sin x}{x(1+x^2)}.
$$

Subtracting $1$ from the numerator leaves this imaginary part unchanged
and removes the apparent pole at the origin. Indeed,

$$
e^{iz}-1=iz+O(z^2),
\qquad g(z)=i+O(z).
$$

Thus $z=0$ is a removable singularity. Define $g(0)=i$ and integrate over
the positively oriented upper semicircular contour $C_R$ shown below.
The real segment passes directly through the origin; no indentation or
principal-value integral is needed.

<img src="/assets/img/blog_figures/20260909_sine_integral_direct_contour.svg" alt="Counterclockwise upper semicircular contour for g(z). The real segment passes through zero, where the removable singularity is filled in by g(0) = i. The simple pole at i is enclosed, and the pole at minus i is outside." style="width: 82%; max-width: 900px; height: auto; display: block; margin: 1.2rem auto;">

The blue point marks the regular extension at $z=0$; the black points
mark the poles. The only pole inside the contour is $z=i$, with residue

$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=i}g(z)
=\left.\frac{e^{iz}-1}{z(z+i)}\right|_{z=i}
=\frac{e^{-1}-1}{-2}
=\frac{1-e^{-1}}2.
$$

On the upper semicircle, $|e^{iz}|\leq1$, so for $R>1$,

$$
|g(z)|\leq\frac{2}{R(R^2-1)}.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\left|\int_{\gamma_R}g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\right|
\leq\frac{2\pi}{R^2-1}\longrightarrow0.
$$

The residue theorem now gives

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\frac{e^{ix}-1}{x(1+x^2)}\,\mathrm{d}x
=2\pi i\operatorname{Res}_{z=i}g(z)
=\pi i(1-e^{-1}).
$$

This real-axis integral is absolutely convergent: $g$ is bounded near
zero and is $O(|x|^{-3})$ at infinity. Taking imaginary parts and using
the evenness of $\sin x/[x(1+x^2)]$ therefore yields

$$
2I=\pi(1-e^{-1}),
\qquad
\boxed{I=\frac{\pi}{2}\left(1-\frac1e\right).}
$$

## Supplementary details

### Why the first differentiation is valid, including at zero

The parameter derivative satisfies

$$
\left|
\frac{\partial}{\partial a}
\frac{\sin(ax)}{x(1+x^2)}
\right|
=\left|\frac{\cos(ax)}{1+x^2}\right|
\leq\frac{1}{1+x^2}.
$$

The bound is integrable and independent of $a$. Dominated convergence
therefore justifies the first differentiation, including the right
derivative at $a=0$, and shows that $F'$ is continuous from the right
there. This validates the initial conditions used in the first method.

### Why the second differentiation is valid for positive parameters

Let $g(x)=x/(1+x^2)$. For $x\geq1$, this function decreases to zero.
Integration by parts gives, for $B>A\geq1$,

$$
\int_A^B g(x)\sin(ax)\,\mathrm{d}x
=\left[-\frac{g(x)\cos(ax)}a\right]_A^B
+\frac1a\int_A^B g'(x)\cos(ax)\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

On any parameter interval $0<\delta\leq a\leq M$, we consequently have

$$
\left|\int_A^B g(x)\sin(ax)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|
\leq\frac{g(A)+g(B)+\int_A^B|g'(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x}{a}
=\frac{2g(A)}a
\leq\frac{2g(A)}\delta
\longrightarrow0.
$$

Thus the derivatives of the truncated integrals defining $F'$ converge
uniformly on every such interval. This justifies differentiating $F'$
under the integral sign for $a>0$.

The restriction matters. Directly setting $a=0$ inside the expression for
$F''$ would give zero, but the formula we found implies

$$
F''(0^+)
=\lim_{h\to0^+}\frac{F'(h)-F'(0^+)}h
=-\frac{\pi}{2}.
$$

There is no contradiction: the tail estimate contains $1/\delta$ and
does not give uniform convergence down to $a=0$. The differential equation
holds for $a>0$, and its initial data come from $F$ and $F'$, whose
integral representations do remain valid at zero.

### A real-variable derivation of the Dirichlet integral

For $\varepsilon>0$, introduce the absolutely convergent integral

$$
D_\varepsilon(b)
=\int_0^\infty e^{-\varepsilon x}\frac{\sin(bx)}x\,\mathrm{d}x,
\qquad b\geq0.
$$

Differentiation under the integral sign and an elementary exponential
integral give

$$
D_\varepsilon'(b)
=\int_0^\infty e^{-\varepsilon x}\cos(bx)\,\mathrm{d}x
=\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon^2+b^2}.
$$

Since $D_\varepsilon(0)=0$,

$$
D_\varepsilon(b)=\arctan\left(\frac b\varepsilon\right)
\longrightarrow\frac{\pi}{2}
\quad (\varepsilon\to0^+,\ b>0).
$$

To connect this limit to the ordinary improper integral, note that
$e^{-\varepsilon x}/x$ decreases to zero. The same integration-by-parts
estimate as above yields

$$
\left|\int_A^\infty
e^{-\varepsilon x}\frac{\sin(bx)}x\,\mathrm{d}x\right|
\leq\frac{2}{bA},\qquad \varepsilon\geq0,\quad b>0.
$$

The tails are therefore uniformly small, including when $\varepsilon=0$.
On $[0,A]$, dominated convergence lets us remove the exponential factor.
Together these facts prove

$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin(bx)}x\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\pi}{2},
\qquad b>0.
$$
