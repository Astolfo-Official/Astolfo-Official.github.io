Consider the integral

$$
I_n=\int_0^\pi\cos(nx)\cos^n x\,\mathrm{d}x,
\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.
$$

We will evaluate it in three ways: integrate the oscillating factor to find
a recurrence, use angle addition to reduce it to a Wallis integral,
and finally use Euler's formula to isolate a constant term. All three give

$$
\boxed{I_n=\frac{\pi}{2^n}.}
$$

For the recurrence, we also include $n=0$, with $I_0=\pi$.
Every integrand below is continuous on its interval, and every sum is
finite, so no convergence or interchange-of-limits argument is needed.
The appendix gives another version of the second method, using a full
multiple-angle expansion and mixed-power Wallis integrals.

## 1. Integration by parts: a recurrence

For $n\geq1$, integrate $\cos(nx)$ and differentiate $\cos^n x$:

$$
u=\cos^n x,
\qquad
\mathrm{d}v=\cos(nx)\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

Then $v=\sin(nx)/n$ and
$\mathrm{d}u=-n\cos^{n-1}x\sin x\,\mathrm{d}x$. Integration by parts gives

$$
I_n=\left[\frac{\cos^n x\sin(nx)}n\right]_0^\pi
+\int_0^\pi\cos^{n-1}x\sin x\sin(nx)\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

The boundary term vanishes because $n$ is an integer. Thus

$$
I_n=\int_0^\pi\cos^{n-1}x\sin x\sin(nx)\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

Apply the product-to-sum identity to the two sine factors:

$$
\sin x\sin(nx)=\frac12\bigl[\cos((n-1)x)-\cos((n+1)x)\bigr].
$$

The integration-by-parts expression therefore becomes

$$
I_n=\frac12\int_0^\pi\cos^{n-1}x
\bigl[\cos((n-1)x)-\cos((n+1)x)\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

Now return to the original integral and factor out one power of cosine:

$$
I_n=\int_0^\pi\cos^{n-1}x\bigl[\cos x\cos(nx)\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

This time, the cosine product-to-sum identity gives a plus sign:

$$
\cos x\cos(nx)=\frac12\bigl[\cos((n-1)x)+\cos((n+1)x)\bigr].
$$

Thus the original expression can also be written as

$$
I_n=\frac12\int_0^\pi\cos^{n-1}x
\bigl[\cos((n-1)x)+\cos((n+1)x)\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

Add the two expressions for $I_n$. Inside the integral, the
$\cos((n+1)x)$ terms cancel, while the $\cos((n-1)x)$ terms add:

$$
2I_n=\frac12\int_0^\pi\cos^{n-1}x
\bigl[2\cos((n-1)x)\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

Canceling the factor of $2$ against $1/2$ leaves exactly the defining
integral for $I_{n-1}$:

$$
2I_n=\int_0^\pi\cos^{n-1}x\cos((n-1)x)\,\mathrm{d}x=I_{n-1}.
$$

We have obtained the recurrence

$$
\boxed{I_n=\frac12 I_{n-1},\qquad n\geq1.}
$$

Starting from $I_0=\pi$ therefore yields

$$
\boxed{I_n=2^{-n}I_0=\frac{\pi}{2^n}.}
$$

## 2. Angle addition: reduction to a Wallis integral

For $n\geq1$, start with the angle-addition formula

$$
\cos(nx)=\cos((n-1)x)\cos x-\sin((n-1)x)\sin x.
$$

Multiplying by $\cos^n x$ and integrating gives

$$
I_n=\int_0^\pi\cos((n-1)x)\cos^{n+1}x\,\mathrm{d}x
-\int_0^\pi\sin((n-1)x)\sin x\cos^n x\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

Integrate the second term by parts, taking

$$
u=\sin((n-1)x),
\qquad v=-\frac{\cos^{n+1}x}{n+1}.
$$

The boundary term vanishes because $\sin((n-1)x)=0$ at both endpoints.
Therefore,

$$
\int_0^\pi\sin((n-1)x)\sin x\cos^n x\,\mathrm{d}x
=\frac{n-1}{n+1}\int_0^\pi\cos((n-1)x)\cos^{n+1}x\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

Substituting back yields

$$
I_n=\frac2{n+1}\int_0^\pi\cos((n-1)x)\cos^{n+1}x\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

Each repetition lowers the frequency by one and raises the cosine power
by one. For $n\geq2$, we can write the successive reductions directly:

$$
I_n=
\frac2{n+1}\cdot\frac4{n+2}
\int_0^\pi\cos((n-2)x)\cos^{n+2}x\,\mathrm{d}x
=\cdots=\frac2{n+1}\cdot\frac4{n+2}\cdots\frac{2n}{2n}
\int_0^\pi\cos^{2n}x\,\mathrm{d}x
=\frac{2^n(n!)^2}{(2n)!}\int_0^\pi\cos^{2n}x\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

For $n=1$, the first reduction already reaches the same final expression.
By symmetry and the Wallis formula,

$$
\int_0^\pi\cos^{2n}x\,\mathrm{d}x
=2\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^{2n}x\,\mathrm{d}x
=\pi\frac{(2n)!}{4^n(n!)^2}.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{I_n=\frac{2^n(n!)^2}{(2n)!}
\frac{\pi(2n)!}{4^n(n!)^2}=\frac\pi{2^n}.}
$$

## 3. Euler's formula: the constant term

Take the real part of a complex integral:

$$
I_n=\operatorname{Re}\int_0^\pi e^{inx}\cos^n x\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

Euler's formula packages both factors into a single power:

$$
e^{inx}\cos^n x=(e^{ix}\cos x)^n
=\left(\frac{1+e^{2ix}}2\right)^n.
$$

Expanding by the binomial theorem gives

$$
I_n=\frac1{2^n}\operatorname{Re}
\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom nk\int_0^\pi e^{2ikx}\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

Every nonconstant exponential integrates to zero: for integers $k\geq1$,

$$
\int_0^\pi e^{2ikx}\,\mathrm{d}x
=\frac{e^{2ik\pi}-1}{2ik}=0.
$$

Only $k=0$ remains, contributing $\pi$. Thus

$$
\boxed{I_n=\frac\pi{2^n}.}
$$

The first method uses product-to-sum identities to combine the original
integral with its integration-by-parts form. The second lowers the frequency while raising the cosine power,
until a single Wallis integral remains. The third makes the
cancellation immediate: all nonconstant complex exponentials vanish on
integration, leaving just the constant coefficient $2^{-n}$.

## Appendix: multiple-angle expansion and generalized Wallis integrals

The Wallis integral used in the second method contains only a power of
cosine. Its mixed-power extension contains powers of both sine and cosine.
For nonnegative integers $a$ and $b$, define

$$
W_{a,b}=\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^{2a}x\cos^{2b}x\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

This is a **generalized Wallis integral**: the ordinary even-power cosine
integral is the special case $a=0$. The mixed-power formula can be viewed
as a fuller version of the Wallis formula, but "generalized" is more
precise than "complete": it extends the family rather than correcting
an incomplete formula. In fact, the beta integral also allows noninteger
powers; the even integer powers here are exactly what this problem needs.

### The mixed-power Wallis formula

For $a\geq1$, integrate by parts with $u=\sin^{2a-1}x$ and
$\mathrm{d}v=\sin x\cos^{2b}x\,\mathrm{d}x$. The boundary term vanishes,
and we obtain

$$
W_{a,b}=\frac{2a-1}{2b+1}W_{a-1,b+1}.
$$

Since $\cos^2x=1-\sin^2x$, we also have
$W_{a-1,b+1}=W_{a-1,b}-W_{a,b}$. Substitution and rearrangement give

$$
W_{a,b}=\frac{2a-1}{2(a+b)}W_{a-1,b}.
$$

The remaining cosine integral obeys the ordinary Wallis recurrence:

$$
W_{0,b}=\frac{2b-1}{2b}W_{0,b-1},
\qquad W_{0,0}=\frac\pi2.
$$

Iterating the two recurrences yields the mixed-power formula

$$
\boxed{W_{a,b}=\frac\pi2
\frac{(2a)!(2b)!}{4^{a+b}a!\,b!\,(a+b)!}.}
$$

### Expanding the multiple angle

Instead of reducing the frequency one step at a time, repeatedly apply
the sine and cosine addition formulas to expand the multiple angle fully:

$$
\cos(nx)=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
(-1)^k\binom n{2k}\cos^{n-2k}x\sin^{2k}x.
$$

The identity follows by induction from the addition formulas, without
complex exponentials. Multiplying by $\cos^n x$ gives

$$
I_n=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(-1)^k\binom n{2k}
\int_0^\pi\cos^{2n-2k}x\sin^{2k}x\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

Each integrand is symmetric about $\pi/2$. Its integral is therefore
$2W_{k,n-k}$, which the mixed-power formula evaluates as

$$
\int_0^\pi\cos^{2n-2k}x\sin^{2k}x\,\mathrm{d}x
=\frac{\pi(2k)!(2n-2k)!}{4^n k!(n-k)!\,n!}.
$$

After multiplying by $\binom n{2k}$ and canceling factorials, we obtain

$$
I_n=\frac\pi{4^n}
\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(-1)^k\binom nk\binom{2n-2k}{n}.
$$

### Evaluating the finite sum

Denote the sum above by $S_n$, and let $[t^n]P(t)$ mean the coefficient
of $t^n$ in a polynomial $P(t)$. For $k>\lfloor n/2\rfloor$, the
coefficient $\binom{2n-2k}{n}$ is zero, so we may extend the sum to $n$:

$$
S_n=[t^n]\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^k\binom nk(1+t)^{2n-2k}.
$$

The binomial theorem collapses the polynomial inside the coefficient:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^k\binom nk(1+t)^{2n-2k}
=\bigl((1+t)^2-1\bigr)^n.
$$

Since $(1+t)^2-1=t(t+2)$, we get

$$
S_n=[t^n]\,t^n(t+2)^n=2^n.
$$

Thus this expanded version of the Wallis method gives the same result:

$$
\boxed{I_n=\frac\pi{4^n}\,2^n=\frac\pi{2^n}.}
$$
